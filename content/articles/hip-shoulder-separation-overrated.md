title: Hip-Shoulder Separation Is Overcoached and Under-Measured
description: Every pitching lesson mentions separation. Almost none of them measure it, and the cue most coaches give actively works against the timing that produces it.
date: 2026-09-05

"Get your hips open before your shoulders" is close to universal as a cue. It's also, on video review, close to backwards for a lot of the pitchers it's given to.

Separation isn't a position you hit. It's a timing gap — how much the pelvis has already rotated toward the plate before the trunk starts to follow it. You can't coach a gap by telling someone to move a body part faster; you coach it by removing whatever is making the trunk rotate *early*, which is almost always a bracing problem in the front leg, not a sequencing problem in the hips.

> Watch the front knee, not the belt. In the pitchers with the least separation on tape, the front leg is still absorbing force when the trunk starts rotating — there's nothing for the pelvis to rotate against yet, so the whole thing fires together instead of in sequence.

The common fix — cueing "hips first" directly — tends to produce one of two failure modes:

1. The pitcher rushes the pelvis early in the delivery, before the arm is in a position to use the separation once it arrives, so the timing gap exists but nothing is loaded against it.
2. The pitcher doesn't change the timing at all, because the actual constraint was never sequencing intent — it was a front leg that collapses instead of blocking, and no verbal cue fixes a strength/stability limitation.

Front-leg block quality is mechanically upstream of separation. A pitcher who can't stabilize a firm front side has no rigid structure for the trunk to rotate against, regardless of how the hips and shoulders are cued. Test the block before you touch the sequencing cue — a broad jump stick test or a single-leg landing assessment will tell you more about whether separation coaching will actually work than another look at the swing-through video will.
