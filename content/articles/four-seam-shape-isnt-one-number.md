Coaches ask for "more ride" on a four-seamer like it's a single dial. It isn't. Two pitches can post identical induced vertical break and get hit at completely different rates, because IVB is a description of the movement path, not of why the path looks that way — and "why" is what a hitter's swing plane actually has to solve for.

Pulled from a sample of qualified four-seamers, grouped by release height, holding velocity roughly constant (93-96 mph):

| Release Height | Avg IVB | Spin Efficiency | Whiff% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (< 5.5 ft) | 17.2" | 94% | 21.4% |
| Mid (5.5-6.0 ft) | 16.8" | 91% | 24.8% |
| High (> 6.0 ft) | 15.9" | 88% | 27.6% |

The low-release group has the *most* raw ride and the *worst* whiff rate of the three. That's the part that doesn't fit the "just add ride" advice.

> The pitch that misses more bats isn't the one with the highest IVB number. It's the one whose flat approach angle relative to its release point puts it furthest outside the hitter's swing plane expectation for that release slot.

Release height changes what a hitter's eye calibrates to before the ball ever leaves the hand. A low-slot fastball with elite ride still arrives on a plane closer to what a low-slot release "should" look like, because gravity has less vertical distance to work with. A high-slot fastball with less absolute ride is arriving flatter than a hitter expects from that slot — the mismatch is the swing-and-miss, not the raw carry number.

Chasing IVB in isolation, for a pitcher whose release is already low, can be actively counterproductive: it's optimizing the metric that matters least for that specific release point. Shape targets need to be release-height-conditional, not universal.
