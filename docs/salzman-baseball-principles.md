# Salzman Baseball — Training Principles

### [strength] 1.1 — Movement analysis of pitching
Pitching is a whole-body, sequential, ballistic, unilateral rotational skill performed at maximal effort and repeated 60–110 times per outing with 15–25 seconds between efforts.

- **Kinetic sequence is proximal to distal.** Pelvis, then trunk, then arm. Training a distal segment without the proximal segments able to deliver to it does not transfer.
- **The lower half has two jobs: timing of rotation, and bracing at landing.** Not magnitude. Peak stride speed occurs early. The lower half's contribution is about *when* rotation is permitted and how well the front side accepts force.
- **The trunk is the largest single contributor to ball velocity.** How the torso moves between foot strike and release is the most controllable variable available.
- **Ground reaction force, measured:** lead leg peak vertical 1.3–2.0× bodyweight; drive leg 0.8–1.2× bodyweight. (MacWilliams et al. 1998; Guido & Werner 2012; ASMI.)
- **Delivery timing, measured:** first movement to foot strike 0.95–1.05 s; foot strike to release 0.14–0.20 s.
- **Hip-to-shoulder separation:** 40–60 degrees.
- **Stride length:** ASMI data for professional pitchers clusters at 80–87% of body height.  

### [strength] 1.2 — The two functional columns
Every delivery splits the body into two columns doing opposite jobs. This distinction drives exercise selection and it drives how asymmetry findings are interpreted.

**ECCENTRIC COLUMN — absorbs, decelerates, resists.**

| Region | Structures |
|---|---|
| Lead leg | Quadriceps (braking at landing), hamstrings, glute max and medius, adductors, anterior tibialis |
| Glove side | Glove-side scapular stabilizers, glove-side shoulder, glove-side obliques |
| Trunk | Trunk decelerators, contralateral obliques, spinal erectors |
| **Throwing arm — posterior** | **Posterior shoulder: infraspinatus, teres minor, posterior deltoid** |
| **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side latissimus dorsi** |
| **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side biceps brachii** (decelerates elbow extension) |
| **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side forearm musculature** (primary shock absorber at the elbow) |

This column is stressed by **braking**, not producing. It does its work after ball release, over roughly 50 milliseconds, and it is where most arm soreness and most deceleration-pattern injury originates.

**CONCENTRIC COLUMN — produces, transfers, accelerates.**

| Region | Structures |
|---|---|
| Trail leg | Glutes, hamstrings, quadriceps, **calves and plantar flexors** (drive and push-off) |
| Hips and trunk | Hip rotators, ipsilateral obliques, spinal erectors into extension |
| **Throwing shoulder** | Subscapularis, **pectoralis major and minor**, latissimus dorsi (as internal rotator), anterior deltoid |
| **Throwing arm** | **Triceps brachii** (elbow extension), serratus anterior |
| Forearm | Pronator teres, wrist flexors (terminal acceleration) |

This column is stressed by **producing**, not absorbing.

**Note on shared structures.** The lat, the quads, and the forearm appear in both columns. That is correct and it is not sloppiness — those structures do both jobs at different instants of the delivery. The lat decelerates the arm after release and contributes to internal rotation during acceleration. The quads brake on the lead side and drive on the trail side. When a structure appears in both columns, **the side determines the column**: lead-leg quad is eccentric, trail-leg quad is concentric.

**Interpretation rule ⚙:** an asymmetry on the eccentric side is a different problem than the same-sized asymmetry on the concentric side. Eccentric-column findings route to landing mechanics, front leg block work, and posterior-chain deceleration capacity. Concentric-column findings route to rotational power and trail-side strength.

### [strength] 1.3 — Physiological analysis — ranked for pitching
| Priority | Quality | Why |
|---|---|---|
| High | Rate of force development | The delivery is too short for peak force to be reached. How fast force rises is the constraint. |
| High | **Anaerobic power and capacity** | The primary energy system of the sport. See 1.4. A pitch is a maximal anaerobic effort, and an inning is a series of them. This is trained, not assumed. |
| High | Eccentric / braking capacity | Front leg block and arm deceleration. Before you can overcome force you must absorb it. |
| High | Rotational power with rotational stability | Speed of rotation must be supported by the ability to stabilize it. Training raw rotational speed on an unstable base raises injury risk instead of transferring. |
| Moderate | Maximal strength | A substrate for the above. Above a working threshold, returns diminish sharply. |
| Moderate | Aerobic capacity | Supports recovery between innings, between outings, and across a season. See 1.4 — this is a real quality, not a concession. |
| Moderate | Hypertrophy | Matters for underweight and younger athletes and for connective tissue capacity. Not a standing goal for a filled-out college arm. |
| Low | Muscular endurance | Exception: forearm, cuff, and scapular tissue tolerance, programmed as tissue work rather than as a performance quality. |

### [strength] 1.4 — Energy system analysis
**Anaerobic is the primary system and is trained as such.**

| System | Role in pitching | Training parameters |
|---|---|---|
| Phosphagen (ATP-PC) | One pitch. ~1.5 s of work with 15–25 s between efforts. | 90–100% intensity, 5–10 s efforts, 1:12 to 1:20 work:rest |
| Fast glycolytic | An inning. Repeated maximal efforts with incomplete recovery. **This is the system that determines whether pitch 18 of an inning looks like pitch 3.** | 75–90% intensity, 15–30 s efforts, 1:3 to 1:5 work:rest |
| Oxidative | Between innings, between outings, across a season. | 20–30% intensity, 3+ min efforts, 1:1 to 1:3 work:rest |
 

What zone 2 actually buys a pitcher:

- **Recovery between outings.** Aerobic fitness governs the rate of phosphocreatine resynthesis and lactate clearance. A starter's ability to be ready again in five days is an aerobic quality.
- **Recovery between innings.** The 10–20 minutes a starter spends in the dugout is aerobic recovery, and how much of the anaerobic system is restored in that window is determined by aerobic capacity.
- **Repeatability within an outing.** Velocity decay from inning one to inning six is partly a conditioning problem. The document already flags "significant change in fastball velocity → endurance/stamina problem" as a diagnostic. This is what trains the thing that diagnostic points at.
- **Cardiac and autonomic adaptation.** Increased stroke volume and improved parasympathetic tone support both recovery and sleep quality.

**The correct framing is minimum effective dose, not exclusion.** Poles are not the problem. Poles as the *only* conditioning, or poles in volumes that compete with power work, are the problem. Programmed as conversational-pace steady state at 20–45 minutes, 2–3×/week, placed on low-CNS days, the interference risk is negligible and the recovery benefit is real.

**Deliberate slow-glycolytic conditioning** — repeated 60–120 second maximal-effort intervals — is the one mode with genuinely poor return for pitchers. It is maximally fatiguing, maximally interfering, and trains a duration the sport never asks for.

### [strength] 2.3 — Countermovement jump — the full model
The phone app measures **one thing**: flight time, from high-frame-rate video. Everything below is derived from it. Implementing the full set costs almost no additional athlete effort and roughly quadruples what the test tells you.

**Jump height from flight time**

```
h = (g × t²) / 8
```

where `g = 9.81 m/s²` and `t` = flight time in seconds. Result in metres. This is the well-validated measurement and it is the foundation for everything else.

**Estimated peak power — Sayers CMJ equation**

```
PP (W) = (60.7 × h_cm) + (45.3 × body_mass_kg) − 2055
PP_per_kg = PP / body_mass_kg
```

**Limits, and they matter.** This is a regression fit on a specific population, not a measurement. Error runs roughly ±5–10%, and it is *systematic* for athletes at the tails of the height and mass distribution — which describes a lot of pitchers. Two consequences: never compare an athlete's estimated PP to published force-plate norms, and only ever chart it against that athlete's own history, where the systematic bias cancels out. Do not use the Lewis formula; it is older and materially less accurate.

**RSI-modified**

```
RSImod = h_m / time_to_takeoff_s
```

Time to takeoff is the interval from the start of the countermovement to the instant of takeoff, read frame by frame. At 240fps each frame is ~4.2 ms, which is adequate resolution.

**This is the highest-value addition to your current battery.** A fatigued athlete frequently *preserves jump height* while time to takeoff lengthens — they compensate with a deeper, slower countermovement and land on the same number. Height alone cannot see this. RSImod can, which makes it the better input to the deload triggers in 4.8.

**Reactive Strength Index — true, from a drop jump**

```
RSI = jump_height_m / ground_contact_time_s
```

Requires a drop jump, which requires the plyometric gate in 3.3. Contact time is read frame by frame. This is the metric that tells you whether KEAT and shock work is actually producing reactive adaptation.

**Eccentric Utilization Ratio**

```
EUR = CMJ_height / SJ_height
```

The squat jump starts from a held 90° position with a 3-second pause and no countermovement. The difference between the two jumps is stretch-shortening cycle contribution.

| EUR | Reading | Training response |
|---|---|---|
| < 1.05 | Minimal SSC contribution | Reactive and plyometric emphasis |
| 1.05–1.15 | Typical | Progress both |
| > 1.15 with low SJ | Elastic but weak underneath | Strength emphasis |
| > 1.15 with high SJ | Well developed | Maintain |

This is the only metric in the battery that directly measures the quality described in the Stretched State principles. It costs one additional test.

**Force-velocity profiling ⚙**

Perform CMJs across 4–6 loads (bodyweight, then roughly +20%, +40%, +60%, +80% of bodyweight as available). For each load, using push-off distance `h_po` measured with a tape from the 90° start position to full extension:

```
F_mean = m × g × ((h / h_po) + 1)      # m = system mass (body + load), kg
v_mean = √(g × h / 2)                  # h = jump height, m
```

Plot `F_mean/m` against `v_mean` across loads. The relationship is linear. Fit it:

- **F0** = y-intercept — theoretical maximal force
- **V0** = x-intercept — theoretical maximal velocity
- **Pmax** = (F0 × V0) / 4
- **FV imbalance** = actual slope compared to the optimal slope for that athlete's Pmax and push-off distance

**Why this is the upgrade that matters most.** Every force-velocity prescription in the prior document was generic — it named the zones but had no way to determine which zone *this* athlete needs. Profiling replaces that guess with the athlete's own data. A force-deficient pitcher and a velocity-deficient pitcher with identical jump heights need opposite blocks, and jump height alone cannot distinguish them.

**Measurement discipline:** push-off distance must be measured the same way every time, and the athlete must hit the same start depth at every load. Inconsistent depth is the dominant error source and will produce a profile that is confidently wrong.

**On predicting velocity from jump data ⚙**

The published jump-to-pitch-velocity relationship is moderate — typically r ≈ 0.4–0.6, explaining roughly a quarter of the variance. That is a real directional signal and a poor point prediction.

Implementation rules:

1. **Do not display a predicted velocity number.** Display percentile-within-roster for each physical metric alongside percentile-within-roster for velocity. The comparison of those two percentiles is the diagnostic.
2. **Log every jump-and-velocity pair** with date, conditions, and athlete. In two to three seasons this roster will support a regression fit on its own athletes, which will beat any borrowed coefficient.
3. **When a model is eventually fit, report a prediction interval, not a point estimate.** A model at r ≈ 0.55 against a population SD of ~4 mph yields a 95% interval near ±6–7 mph. Showing the interval is what stops the number from being read as a target.
4. **A multi-predictor model beats any single metric.** Candidate inputs: CMJ height, RSImod, rotational med ball throw distance, body mass, standing height, F0, V0. Collect all of them now even before the model exists.

### [strength] 3.3 — Readiness gates
Gates are pass/fail entry criteria, not goals. An athlete who has not passed the gate does not get the exercise, regardless of phase. Gate status is stored per athlete and re-verified every 8 weeks.

| Gate | Criterion | How verified remotely |
|---|---|---|
| **G1 — Loaded jumps, advanced plyometrics** | Trap bar deadlift 1.5× bodyweight for 3 at RPE ≤8, **or** front squat at bodyweight for 5 at RPE ≤8 | Video of the qualifying set |
| **G2 — Single-leg plyometrics** | Single-leg stand, quarter squat, and half squat held 30 s each, per side, without loss of position | Video, one continuous clip per side |
| **G3 — Depth jumps and KEAT** | G1 and G2 both passed, plus 4 weeks of progressive plyometric exposure completed, plus no current asymmetry flag (>10%) on any lower-body bilateral test | Video of a 12-inch drop jump showing a quiet landing, no valgus collapse, no heel slam, contact time under 250 ms |
| **G4 — Drop heights above 18 inches** | G3 passed, bodyweight under 220 lb, RSI stable or improving at the current height for 2 consecutive sessions | Video at current height before progression is granted |

**G3 and G4 exist because these methods are trained without a coach present.** The video submission is not optional and is not a formality — it is the only mechanism verifying that the landing, which is the entire stimulus, is being performed correctly. An athlete who does not submit video does not progress in drop height, and a landing that degrades pulls the athlete back one height increment.

### [strength] 4.5 — Exercise selection and order
**Classes.** *Core* exercises are multi-joint and take priority. *Assistance* exercises are single-joint, generally corrective. *Structural* exercises load the spine directly or indirectly. *Power* exercises are structural movements performed explosively. *Recovery* exercises are low-stress and sit at the end of a session or in a session of their own.

**Order within a session:** power → core strength → assistance → recovery. Alternate upper/lower or push/pull to manage fatigue.

**Front-loading rule:** corrective, CNS, power, and agility work is performed **before** weight training and throwing, not after. Transfer is better when these are frontloaded.

**Muscle balance:** the agonist should not significantly outpace the antagonist. For pitchers this is specifically the accelerator/decelerator relationship at the shoulder, which is why posterior shoulder and scapular work is standing content rather than a phase.

**Cross-pattern selection:** force transfers contralaterally — for a right-handed pitcher, right pec and shoulder girdle work with left oblique, hip flexor, and quad, while left lat and right glute work together. A useful lens when selecting anti-rotation and rotational accessory work.

### [strength] 4.6 — Frequency — the CNS budget
Frequency is not set by lifting alone. **The governing number is total high-CNS exposures per week, and throwing counts.**

**High-CNS:** max-effort lifting (RPE 9+), sprinting, extensive plyometrics, depth jumps and KEAT, ballistic lower-body work, any high-intent throwing day, game appearances.

**Low-CNS:** submaximal lifting (RPE ≤7), crawling and positional work, moderate horizontal jumps, mobility, low-intensity isometrics, zone 2 aerobic work, recovery sessions.

**Standing rules:**

1. Never stack two high-CNS days back to back.
2. Throwing days are high-CNS days when planning lifting.
3. High-CNS lifting does not precede a high-intent throwing day.
4. **An athlete's team lift heavy day is a high-CNS exposure we do not control.** Ask once, store it on the athlete record, treat it as fixed for the season, and program around it.
5. Weekly high-CNS ceiling: 3 off-season, 2 in-season (which for most athletes is consumed entirely by throwing and team lifting).

### [strength] 4.7 — One primary quality per phase
A phase develops one primary quality. Everything else is maintained, not developed. Two primary qualities produces two half-adaptations and a fatigued athlete.

The primary quality is chosen from the 1.3 ranking and the force-velocity profile from 2.3, and does not change until the mesocycle boundary (2–6 weeks, standard 4) — testing and quality changes happen at block boundaries only, never mid-block.

### [strength] 4.8 — Progression, deload, and regression
**The RPE 2-for-2 rule.** If a prescribed RPE 8 set comes back at RPE 6 or lower for two consecutive sessions, increase load: 2–5% upper body, 5–10% lower body. The reverse also holds — RPE 10 on a prescribed 8 for two consecutive sessions drops the load and triggers a deload check.

**Deload triggers.** Any two in the same week:

- CMJ height down ≥5% from rolling baseline
- **RSImod down ≥10% from rolling baseline** (more sensitive than height; often fires first)
- Prescribed loads coming in 2+ RPE points higher than programmed
- Resting heart rate elevated ≥5 bpm over baseline for 3+ days
- Reported sleep disruption across multiple nights
- Reported loss of appetite or persistent mood disturbance
- Arm feeling heavy or slow during warm-up on consecutive throwing days
- Velocity down with no mechanical change identified

**Text check-in routing ⚙.** Reporting is informal, so common phrasings route directly:

| What they say | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| "Arm felt heavy warming up" | Incomplete recovery from prior throwing | Drop intent one level. No high-CNS lift that day. |
| "Couldn't finish the last set" | Load mis-set or fatigue accumulating | Check against the reverse 2-for-2 rule. |
| "Dead legs" | Lower-body fatigue, often team lift | Pull ballistic lower-body work. Keep throwing. |
| "Shoulder sore in front" | Mechanics issue | Pull high intent. Review video. |
| "Shoulder or elbow sore in back" | Accelerator/decelerator imbalance | Pull high intent. Audit posterior shoulder and eccentric-column volume. |
| "Elbow sore on the inside" | Medial elbow — treat as serious | Stop throwing. Assess before any return. |
| "Slept badly all week" | Systemic | Count toward deload trigger. |
| "Felt great, want more" | — | Not a reason to add volume. Progress per 2-for-2 only. |

### [strength] 5.2 — The governing theory
**Stimulus–fatigue–recovery–adaptation.** The greater the workload, the greater the fatigue and the longer the delay before adaptation appears. As fatigue dissipates, performance rises above baseline. If no new stimulus arrives, detraining follows.

**Fitness–fatigue.** Every session produces both fitness and fatigue. Preparedness is the difference between them. High loads raise fitness *and* fatigue simultaneously, so preparedness drops before it rises. This is why a peak cannot be held: peak performance is sustainable for 1–2 weeks, and attempting to hold it longer produces either fitness loss or overtraining.

**Overtraining continuum:**

| State | Duration | Signs | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute fatigue | 1–2 days | None, or improved performance | Normal. Continue. |
| Functional overreaching | Days to 1 week | Temporary performance dip | Deload week resolves it. |
| Non-functional overreaching | Weeks to months | Stagnant or declining performance, mood disturbance, degraded coordination | Extended unload. Reassess the whole block. |
| Overtraining syndrome | Months to years | Dramatic performance loss, frequent illness | Refer out. Not a programming problem at this point. |

Causes: large jumps in volume or intensity, and **training monotony** — the same stimulus repeated without variation, which is the failure mode most common in remote programming.

### [strength] 5.5 — Non-linear structure for advanced athletes
This roster is advanced, so linear progression within a block is exhausted. Within a microcycle, vary intensity: one heavy day and one speed day in the same week rather than progressing both together. Light days carry roughly 80% of the heavy day's load.

**Block structure ⚙:** standard mesocycle is 4 weeks — three loading weeks with an ascending profile, then a deload. Deload week drops volume to roughly 50% and holds intensity, which sheds fatigue without shedding fitness.

### [strength] 6.1 — Eccentric training
**Classifications:**

| Class | Load | Method | Available remotely |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submaximal | 60–90% of concentric max | Slow eccentrics, tempo work, isometric/eccentric contrast | Yes |
| Supramaximal | 105–140% of concentric max | 2:1 tempo (lower twice as slowly as you lift), pure eccentrics | **Only with a competent partner or weight releasers** |
| Above 140% | — | Requires flywheel or accommodating resistance | **Not prescribed.** Not achievable with a barbell; attempting it is a control problem, not a stimulus. |

Percentages are of the athlete's **concentric** 1RM for that lift. Anything above 100% is by definition supramaximal.

**Remote default:** tempo eccentrics at submaximal load. A 4-second lowering phase at 70% produces real eccentric stimulus with no partner requirement and no failure exposure. This is the workhorse and it should be the default prescription, with supramaximal work reserved for athletes who confirm a training partner.

**Programming:** 1–2 eccentric-emphasis exercises per session, in the strength phases. Eccentric work carries a long recovery cost — 48–72 hours minimum before the same pattern is loaded again, and it does not belong in the week before a start.

### [strength] 6.2 — Isometric training
| Type | Application |
|---|---|
| Overcoming | Push against an immovable object. Maximal neural drive. |
| Yielding | Hold against gravity. Strength-endurance and position tolerance. |

| Classification | Prescription | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Max duration | 20–60 s at 50–80% | Hypertrophy, tissue tolerance |
| Max intensity | 6–10 s at maximal intent | Neural drive |
| Ballistic | 1–2 s explosive effort | RFD |

**Ramp-up isometric protocol, applied to throwing positions:**

| Tier | Effort | Hold | Reps | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50% | 15 s | 2 | Prime and perfuse |
| 2 | 75% | 7 s | 2 | Cross the threshold |
| 3 | 90% | 4 s | 2 | Near-max intent |

15–30 seconds rest between reps. Applied to each throwing position: layback (ER), release point (IR), elbow flexion, elbow extension — all at 90 degrees of abduction, with the release-point position done for IR only.

Isometrics are low-CNS and can be programmed on days that would otherwise be off. They are the most useful modality for the E3 equipment tier.

### [strength] 6.3 — Ballistic training
**Load:** 10–25% of max for projected implements. The load is projected rather than decelerated, which is the defining feature.

**Methods:** med ball throws, jump squats, weighted jumps, rotational throws.

**Governing rule:** implement speed is the prescription. The set ends when speed drops, not when the rep count is met.

**Volume:** 3–6 sets of 3–5 reps per exercise. Full recovery between sets. Ballistic work done under fatigue trains the wrong thing.

### [strength] 6.4 — Plyometric training
**Definition:** maximum force in the shortest possible time. Trains the stretch-shortening cycle, the series elastic components, the tendons, and the stretch reflex.

**The stretch-shortening cycle:**

1. **Eccentric phase** — the agonist is stretched, elastic energy is stored in the series elastic components
2. **Amortization phase** — the transition. Signals transmit to the agonist. **The longer this phase, the more stored energy dissipates as heat.** Amortization time is the whole game.
3. **Concentric phase** — muscle shortens, elastic energy is released

**Throwing is itself a plyometric action.** Plyometric throwing volume counts against plyometric volume caps below.

**Volume caps — foot contacts or throws per session:**

| Training status | Contacts/session |
|---|---|
| Beginner | 80–100 |
| Intermediate | 100–120 |
| Advanced | 120–140 |

**Block length:** 6–10 weeks. Progressively increase frequency, volume, intensity, and exercise type across the block. Plyometrics are a block modality, not standing content.

**Pairing rule:** combine lower-body lifting with upper-body plyometrics, and upper-body lifting with lower-body plyometrics. Heavy lifting and plyometrics for the same region on the same day is complex training — it works, but it demands recovery this roster's throwing volume does not leave room for. **Not prescribed in-season.**

### [strength] 6.5 — KEAT and shock training
**Kinetic Energy Accumulation Training** uses falling or absorbed forces to develop supramaximal eccentric force. Methods: depth jumps, altitude landings, overspeed eccentrics with bands or releases.

**Why it stays in the system.** Removing the Olympic lifts removes the catch phase, which was one source of force-absorption training. KEAT trains force absorption more directly and at higher magnitude than a clean catch does, and it is the primary trainer of braking rate of force development — the eccentric column's defining quality and one of the better predictors of pitching velocity in the jump literature.

> **"Before you can overcome force, you must absorb it."**

**Entry requirements:** G3 passed (see 3.3). No exceptions, no partial credit, no "he's been lifting a long time."

**Progression ⚙:**

| Step | Drop height | Advancement criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Altitude landing only (no rebound), 12" | 3 sessions, quiet landing on video, no valgus |
| 2 | Depth jump, 12" | RSI stable or improving across 2 sessions |
| 3 | Depth jump, 18" | RSI stable or improving across 2 sessions; G4 for anything above |
| 4 | Depth jump, 24" | Bodyweight under 220 lb; RSI must **improve** over the 18" value or the athlete stays at 18" |

**Hard limits:**

- Bodyweight over 220 lb: do not exceed 18 inches.
- Ground contact time above 250 ms means the athlete is absorbing rather than rebounding — drop the height.
- RSI that falls when height rises means the height is too high. **The optimal drop height is the tallest box at which RSI is still improving, and it is individual.** This is why RSI is worth measuring.
- Maximum 40 contacts per session for depth jumps specifically, counted inside the 6.4 volume cap.
- High-CNS. Never within 48 hours of a start, never stacked with another high-CNS day.
- Video submission required at every height progression. No video, no progression.

**Placement:** first transition phase (November–December) and, if the calendar allows, a shorter block in the general preparatory period. **Not in-season.**

### [strength] 6.6 — Weighted implement throwing
**Load range: ±20% of a regulation 5 oz baseball. That means 4 oz and 6 oz. Nothing outside that window.**

**Basis.** The ±20% under/overload window traces to DeRenne's work on weighted-implement throwing, which found velocity gains from paired under/overload training within a narrow load range. Two independent lines of evidence support the narrow window:

1. Loading heavier than 6 oz when training for arm speed specifically is unlikely to help arm speed, mechanical efficiency, or joint health.
2. The sprint literature finds that external load which reduces movement velocity by more than 10–12% becomes counterproductive for developing speed. The same logic applies to throwing, and 7 oz exceeds that threshold.

> **Supersession notice.** This replaces the 3 oz / 7 oz protocol carried in the Hacking the Kinetic Chain working notes. Those loads sit at ±40% and are outside the supported window. Any template referencing 3 oz or 7 oz implements is out of date and should be rebuilt against this section.

**Testing protocol.** Establish min, max, and average velocity for each implement:

| Implement | Throws |
|---|---|
| 5 oz baseball | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE |
| 6 oz | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE |
| 5 oz baseball | 2 @ 100% RPE |
| 4 oz | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE |

**Training protocol.** Same sequence, 3–4 throws at 100% RPE per implement. **Terminate the session early when velocity drops 4% below the tested average.** This is the auto-regulation rule and it is not optional — it is what keeps a velocity session from becoming a fatigue session.

**Frequency:** 2–3× per 14 days in a velocity block. Never in-season for a starter without removing an equivalent high-intent exposure elsewhere.

### [strength] 6.7 — Rotational work
**Stability precedes speed.** Speed of rotation must be supported by the ability to stabilize rotation first. Training raw rotational speed without adequate core and base stability increases injury risk rather than transferring to velocity. Anti-rotation work is the entry requirement for rotational power work, not an accessory to it.

**Progression:** anti-rotation (pallof press, bird dog) → controlled rotation under load (chops, lifts) → rotational power (med ball throws) → rotational power from throwing-specific positions.

**Diagonal chop:** performed across a progression of increasingly demanding stance positions that mimic release stance. Trains the body to stabilize the speeds and forces produced in the arms during rotation. Cable at E1/E2 where available; band substitution at E2/E3 is mandatory rather than optional.

### [throwing] 10.1 — Delivery phases
| Phase | Boundaries | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Windup / gather | First movement → max leg lift | **First movement off-balances the center of gravity.** That controlled fall is what creates initial momentum, and it drives torso counter-rotation. Nothing is thrown yet, but the delivery's direction and rhythm are set here. |
| Stride | Leg lift → foot strike | **Where momentum genuinely accelerates.** Directional momentum builds, hip-shoulder separation reaches its maximum, and the athlete converts the fall into travel down the mound. |
| Late cocking | Foot strike → maximum external rotation | **Peak joint loading.** At foot strike the hips are rotated by ground contact — the lead leg hits and the ground turns the pelvis. Rotation is received from the ground, not initiated by the hips. Energy transfers up the chain from here. |
| Acceleration | MER → ball release | Arm speed. 0.14–0.20 s total. |
| Deceleration | Release → maximum internal rotation | Braking. Where the eccentric column (§1.2) does its work. |
| Follow-through | Deceleration → balanced finish | Dissipation of remaining momentum. |

**Why the foot strike point matters for coaching:** if the hips are rotated by ground contact rather than by voluntary hip action, then cues telling an athlete to "fire the hips" are asking him to do actively what the ground is going to do to him anyway — and doing it early costs him the separation built during the stride. The trainable variable is *when he lands and how well he accepts the ground*, not how hard he turns.

The two phases that matter most for training are **late cocking** (where load peaks) and **deceleration** (where capacity is tested). Most drill work targets the phases on either side of foot strike because that is where the athlete has usable control.

### [throwing] 10.2 — What is trainable and what is individual
This distinction is load-bearing. "Continuously refining" without it becomes churn.

**Trainable — a legitimate target for change:**

- Timing and sequencing (when rotation begins relative to foot strike)
- Stride direction and foot strike alignment
- Lead leg action at and after foot strike
- Rhythm and tempo consistency
- Arm timing relative to the lower half
- Posture and head stability through release
- Glove-side action

**Individual — not a target for change absent a symptom:**

- **Arm slot.** Perceived arm slot is largely a function of lateral trunk tilt, not shoulder abduction. Shoulder abduction at release sits near 90° in nearly every pitcher regardless of how high or low the slot appears. **"Raise your arm slot" is almost always a trunk tilt instruction wearing the wrong name.** If you want the slot to change, say what you actually mean and understand it comes with a trade-off.
- Leg kick height and gather style
- Tempo preference, as long as it's repeatable
- Arm action shape, absent a flag from §17
- Anything currently producing output with no symptom

**The burden of proof sits on the change.** Continuous refinement is a bias toward improvement, not a license to touch everything. A pattern that is unusual, repeatable, painless, and productive is not a problem to solve.

### [throwing] 12.1a — What reduced effort actually delivers — and why it changes the volume math
Pitchers do not scale down the way the instruction implies, and this is one of the better-replicated findings in the throwing literature.

| Instruction | Ball velocity produced | Elbow varus torque produced |
|---|---|---|
| "50% effort" | ~78–86% of max | ~75–87% of max |
| "75% effort" | ~86–90% of max | ~81–93% of max |

Sources: Melugin, Larson, Fleisig, Conte, Fealy, Dines, D'Angelo & Camp (*OJSM*, 2019) — 60 HS and collegiate pitchers, motus sleeve, 120 ft long toss. Hyeamang, Dowling, Hodakowski et al. (*OJSM*, 2026) — 38 HS and 24 professional pitchers, 3D motion capture at 480 Hz. Both found perceived effort correlated strongly with measured torque (R² > 0.85) while the *magnitude* of reduction never matched the instruction. Roughly: every 25% drop in stated effort buys about 7% less torque and 11% less velocity.

**Three consequences for this system ⚙**

1. **Throw count, not intent, is the unit of arm-load accounting.** Sixty drill throws at I2 delivers most of the elbow load of sixty throws at I5. Low intent buys mechanical control and attentional bandwidth. It does not buy an unloaded arm.
2. **Drill volume must be counted.** Every throw above catch-play distance enters the weekly budget in §15.2 regardless of the intent it was prescribed at. A movement-change block is not free.
3. **Return-to-throw cannot be controlled by effort percentage alone** (§18.3). Distance, throw count, and rest interval are the controllable variables. "Throw at 50%" is an instruction, not a dose.

**What low intent is still for:** motor learning. A pattern installed at I2 is installed because attention is available, not because the arm is protected. Both things are true at once and the document should say so.

### [throwing] 12.3 — Gates — what has to be true before a guy is allowed to do something
Four checkpoints for throwing, same spirit as the G1-G4 lifting gates in §3.3. Each is a question with a yes-or-no answer. If the answer is no, he doesn't do the thing yet.

**T1 — High-intent throwing (I4 or I5):**
- No red flag currently active from §17 (pain, velocity loss, location loss)
- Shoulder rotational ROM asymmetry within normal limits (assessed via Arm Care testing)
- Completed an on-ramp — throwing consistently, not coming off a layoff

**T2 — Weighted implements:**
- T1 passed
- At least 4 weeks of steady throwing at moderate intent or higher

Weighted implements are not how you get someone back into throwing shape. They're for an arm that's already in shape.

**T3 — Pulldowns or velocity testing:**
- T1 and T2 passed
- It's the off-season, or he's in a designated velocity block

Not during the season. A pulldown day and a start in the same week is two max-effort exposures.

**T4 — Installing a new mechanical change at high intent:**
- The change has already gotten through Stage 3 (§13.5)

You can't teach a new pattern and throw hard at the same time. The pattern has to survive mixed practice at moderate intent first.

### [throwing] 13.5 — The four stages of a change
Every mechanical change goes through four stages. The point of splitting it up is that **a change that looks good early usually isn't finished** — it just looks finished, because the conditions are easy.

---

**Stage 1 — Learning the shape.**
One drill, done over and over. Low intent. You give feedback on every rep. This is where he figures out what the movement even feels like. A body-part cue is fine here, because he has to know what's moving before he can chase a result.

**Stage 2 — Making it repeatable.**
Same drill, still repeated, intent creeping up. You start talking less — comment only when a rep misses. Switch to an external cue now that he knows the shape.

**Stage 3 — Making it survive a mess.**
Now you mix things up. Different drills in the same session, different pitches, nothing repeated in a neat block. Intent goes up.

**His performance will get worse here. Tell him that before it happens.** Mixed practice feels worse than blocked practice — that's the whole reason it works. Without the warning, the drop reads as failure and guys abandon a change one stage before it would have stuck.

**Stage 4 — Proving it on the mound.**
Full delivery, real intent, bullpens and games. **You say nothing.** No cue, no reminder, no "remember to..."

This is the only place you find out whether the change is real.

---

| Stage | What it looks like | Intent | How much you talk | Cue type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Shape | One drill, repeated | I2 | Every rep | Body part OK |
| 2 — Repeatable | Same drill, more intent | I3 | Only on misses | External |
| 3 — Mixed | Drills and pitches shuffled | I4 | After the block, not during | External, rarely |
| 4 — Mound | Full delivery, competing | I4–I5 | **Nothing** | **None** |

---

**The reason it's built this way:** repeating one drill makes the movement look great during practice and makes it *less* likely to hold up later. Mixing it up makes practice look worse and makes it stick. That trade is the single most useful thing in the motor learning literature and it's counterintuitive enough that it's worth restating to athletes.

**So: the drill is not the evidence. The mound is the evidence.** A guy who looks perfect in Stage 1 has told you almost nothing.

### [throwing] 13.8 — Intent and movement change
A pattern acquired at I2 does not automatically exist at I5. Motor solutions are speed-specific — the nervous system solves the problem differently when the movement gets faster, and a pattern that was never tested at speed has not been installed, only rehearsed.

**Rules:**

1. Movement change work happens at reduced intent — Stage 1 is capped at I2, Stage 2 at I3.
2. Intent rises through the stages. It is a variable in the progression, not a constant.
3. **Never attempt to install a new pattern at competitive intent.** It will not take, and the attempt costs command.
4. A change is not real until it survives I4+ without a cue.

### [throwing] 14.1 — Direction and structure
**Simple → Complex | Slow → Fast | Constrained → Unconstrained | Low DOF → High DOF**

The seven phases are a practical application of block, random, and deep practice. Constrain and Time are block training — 1–2 controlled variables, isolating a problem so the nervous system can solve it without interference. Adapt, Move, Bounce, and Transfer progressively introduce the uncontrolled variables of random training. Compete is deep practice: full expression, no constraints remaining.

### [throwing] 14.2 — The phases are layers, not a ladder
**An athlete is not "in" one phase.** The routing in §14.3 decides which phase gets the *emphasis* — the volume and the attention — while other phases continue running underneath it.

**Concurrency rules:**

- **Phases 3, 4, and 5 run even while Phase 1 is the emphasis.** Adapt, Move, and Bounce are not rewards for completing Constrain. An athlete working on arm action still needs momentum, variability, and lead-leg exposure or he loses them while he fixes one thing.
- **Phase 6 is folded into Phase 4.** Transfer work is momentum work performed from game positions. It is not a separate stage; it is a subset of Move for athletes who field.
- **Phase 7 is not just in-season.** Compete belongs in the **late off-season** as well — the first transition period is where a change has to prove it survives full intent, and that requires competitive throwing before February.

**What the exit criteria actually govern:** not whether the phase is *available*, but whether the phase can stop being the emphasis. An athlete who hasn't met the Phase 1 exit keeps Constrain as his emphasis; he still does Move and Bounce work at maintenance volume.

|---|---|

| Compete (catch play, flat ground, or bullpen) | 10–20% |

### [throwing] 14.3 — Problem-based routing
| Finding | Emphasize |
|---|---|
| Inconsistent arm action | Phase 1 — Constrain |
| Poor timing or rushing | Phase 2 — Time |
| Mechanical rigidity | Phase 3 — Adapt |
| Low velocity or stiff delivery | Phase 4 — Move |
| Soft lead leg or poor force transfer | Phase 5 — Bounce |
| Position player skills | Phase 6 — Transfer |
| In-season | Phase 7 — Compete |

**"Emphasize" does not mean "run exclusively."** A session normally touches 2–4 phases; the routing determines which one gets the volume and the attention, not which one gets performed.

**Default, with no specific finding on record.** A pitcher with no logged mechanical issue and no active change (§13.7) is not "in" any phase by default. **Emphasize Phase 4 — Move.** It is the general momentum/sequencing/force-transfer phase, not a remedial one — Phase 1 (Constrain) is for a specific, named arm-action problem, not a default starting point. Do not route a healthy, no-finding pitcher into Phase 1 absent an actual reason to.

### [throwing] 14.4 — Session flow
**Arm Preparation → Constraint Drills → Timing Drills → Adaptability Drills → Momentum Drills → Plyometric Drills → Long Toss (optional) → Flat Ground or Bullpen → Arm Recovery Protocol**

Movement work precedes intent work. A pattern is installed at low intent and then expressed, never the reverse.

### [throwing] 14.5 — Long toss
**Auto-regulated. Athletes are not thrown to predetermined distances.**

**Going out:** increase distance progressively while maintaining athletic movement, fluid arm action, proper arc, good intent, and acceptable recovery.

**Coming in:** high-intent line drives from maximum distance back toward the target.

**Max distance is defined as the furthest the athlete throws while maintaining quality movement** — not the furthest he can reach.

**Stop increasing distance when any of the following appear:** mechanics deteriorate, effort compensation becomes visible, accuracy drops excessively, or pain or discomfort occurs.

**Role separation:** long toss develops physical throwing capacity. Flat grounds develop skill. Bullpens develop competition readiness. All three complement each other — none substitutes for another, and a program heavy in one is not covered for the others.

### [throwing] 14.6 — Velocity development — what actually drives it
**The mechanism, stated honestly.** The cause of weighted-ball velocity gain is not settled. Driveline's own published work states there is no conclusive evidence explaining it and that the "builds arm strength" explanation is likely wrong. What the evidence does support:

1. **Increased shoulder external rotation (layback).** Reinold et al. (2018) ran a 6-week weighted-ball program: velocity rose, and so did passive shoulder ER. **4 of 17 in the training group sustained elbow injuries** — 2 during the program, 2 the following season — against 0 in the control group. Greater layback lengthens the acceleration path and stores more elastic energy. The adaptation producing the velocity is the same adaptation producing the risk — a lever, not a free gain.
2. **Altered arm kinematics.** Weighted implements shift maximum shoulder internal rotation velocity, elbow extension velocity, and the timing of max external rotation. Internal rotation and elbow extension are the primary contributors to ball velocity, so the implements are training the right variables.

**So the gain is partly neural and partly tissue, and the tissue part carries the injury.** Any program using weighted implements should be monitoring shoulder ROM specifically, because that is the adaptation being induced.

### [throwing] 14.7 — Constrained positions do not reduce arm stress
**This corrects a widely held assumption, including one previously carried in this document.**

The intuition is that removing the lower half lowers the load on the arm. The measurements say otherwise:

- **Towel drills** — a more constrained task than any throw in Phase 1 — produced elbow valgus torque reaching **roughly 80% of full-effort active throwing.**
- **Squatting throws in catchers** showed **no difference** in medial elbow torque compared with standing throws.
- **Kibler & Chandler:** a 20% reduction in kinetic energy transfer from hip and trunk requires a **34% increase in shoulder rotational velocity** to produce equivalent arm force.

**Removing the legs does not unload the arm. It transfers the demand to the arm.**

**Consequences ⚙**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Two-knee and one-knee throws are low-stress | **False.** They are low-*velocity*, which is not the same thing. |
| Constrained drills are a safe way to add volume | **False.** They enter the §15.2 ledger at full weight. |
| You can build top-end arm speed from a constrained position | **No.** The position caps producible arm speed, and arm speed you cannot produce is arm speed you cannot train. |
| Constrained positions are valuable | **Yes — for motor learning.** They free attention and reduce degrees of freedom. That is the entire benefit and it is a real one. |

This is the same finding as §12.1a reached by a different route: **the things that feel like they lower arm load mostly lower velocity instead.**

### [throwing] 14.8 — Dry reps
**The one genuinely low-load rehearsal tool.** A dry rep is the delivery performed with no ball, no implement, and **no violent deceleration** — the arm is not whipped through and released. That last condition is what separates a dry rep from a towel drill, and it is why the towel drill finding above does not apply.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Use for** | Stage 1 shape acquisition (§13.5), between-session rehearsal, in-season pattern maintenance, Protective-phase return-to-throw |
| **Arm load** | Negligible when performed without a whipped finish |
| **Counts toward §15.2** | **No** |
| **Dosage** | 5–15 reps, daily if useful |
| **Intent** | Deliberately submaximal. A dry rep taken to full speed is a towel drill. |

Dry reps are the cheapest motor-learning volume available and are underused. They are the correct tool when an athlete needs repetitions of a new shape and has no arm-load budget left in the week.

### [throwing] 14.9 — Med ball work in the throwing program
Rotational med ball throws are programmed under §6.7 as rotational power. Within the throwing program they serve a second function: **producing rotational velocity and sequencing at high intent with no elbow valgus exposure.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Use for** | Rotational power, sequencing, intent expression on non-throwing days |
| **Counts toward §15.2** | **No** — no throwing-arm valgus load |
| **Counts toward §6.4 plyometric volume** | **Yes** |
| **Dosage** | 3–5 sets × 3–5 throws per side |
| **Placement** | Can carry high intent on days where arm-load budget is spent |

**This is the answer to "how do I train intent without spending arm."** Med ball work is the only high-intent modality in the system that does not enter the arm ledger.

### [throwing] 14.10 — Prescribing velocity work intelligently
Three modalities develop top-end velocity. They are not interchangeable and they are not equally risky.

| Modality | Trains | Arm cost | Constraints |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High-intent long toss** | Arm speed, whole-chain output | High | I4. Auto-regulated per §14.5. |
| **Weighted implements (4/5/6 oz)** | IR velocity, elbow extension velocity, layback | High, and induces the ROM change in §14.6 | §6.6 protocol. 4% termination rule. Monitor shoulder ROM. |
| **Pulldowns** | Peak velocity expression | **Lower than assumed** — ASMI and Driveline both found slower arm speed in pulldowns than mound pitching, at comparable elbow torque | Gate T3 (§12.3). Off-season or velocity block only. |

**On pulldowns specifically.** The assumption that pulldowns are the highest-stress throw is not supported. The run-up's linear momentum appears to substitute for arm speed rather than add to it. **The risk in pulldowns is volume and frequency, not the movement.**

**Intelligent pulldown rules ⚙**

1. Gate T3 required (§12.3) — off-season or a designated velocity block only, never in-season.
2. Maximum **1 pulldown session per 14 days.**
3. Maximum **8–12 max-effort pulldowns per session**, after a full long-toss build-up.
4. Terminate on the §6.6 rule: 4% below tested average.
5. Never within 48 h of another high-CNS exposure.
6. **Never the primary velocity modality.** Pulldowns measure and express velocity. Long toss and weighted implements build it.
7. Shoulder ROM re-checked within 72 h of any pulldown or weighted-implement session.

**The honest summary:** you cannot build top-end velocity in a low-stress position, because the velocity and the stress arise from the same thing — high arm speed. What you can do is control how often you visit it, terminate on fatigue rather than rep count, and monitor the ROM change that is the actual mechanism of both the gain and the risk.

### [throwing] 15.1 — The variables
Decided in this order: **throw count → intent distribution → distance → implement → frequency → recovery interval.**

Throw count comes first because it is the variable that actually governs arm load (§12.1a). Intent is decided second, and it governs what the session is *for* — not how much it costs.

### [throwing] 15.2 — Arm load accounting
**The unit is a throw, weighted by implement. Not intent** (§12.1a).

**Counts:** every throw beyond warm-up catch distance — drill throws, constrained-position throws, long toss, flat grounds, bullpens, competitive pitches, pulldowns.

**Does not count:** warm-up catch inside 60 feet at I1, dry reps (§14.8), med ball work (§14.9), non-throwing arm care.

**Implements — 5 oz standard, no plyo balls**

| Implement | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| 5 oz (standard) | 1.0 |
| 4 oz | 1.1 |
| 6 oz | 1.1 |
| Football, javelin, softball, frisbee (Phase 3) | 0.8 |

**Constrained-position throws count at full weight** (1.0), per §14.7. A two-knee throw is not a discounted throw.

### [throwing] 15.2a — Where the weekly budget comes from — and why it isn't a number
**Straight answer: there is no published weekly throw budget for college pitchers, and any absolute number offered here would be invented.** Pitch-count guidelines that do exist (Pitch Smart and similar) are per-outing limits for youth and high school arms, derived from injury-surveillance work at those ages. They do not extend to a college pitcher's total weekly throwing across drills, long toss, and bullpens, and nobody has established what that ceiling is.

So the system does not use an absolute ceiling. **It uses a rate-of-change rule instead**, which requires knowing only the athlete's own history:

```
acute_load   = weighted throws, trailing 7 days
chronic_load = average weekly weighted throws, trailing 28 days
ratio        = acute_load / chronic_load
```

| Ratio | Reading | Action |
|---|---|---|
| < 0.8 | Undertrained relative to recent history | Load can rise |
| 0.8 – 1.3 | Appropriate progression | Proceed |
| 1.3 – 1.5 | Spike forming | Flag. Hold volume flat next week. |
| > 1.5 | Sharp spike | Reduce. Do not add intent this week. |

**Why this is better than a number I made up:** it needs no population norm, it is athlete-relative, and it directly targets the thing that actually tracks with injury — a rapid increase relative to what the athlete is conditioned for, rather than a high absolute total. A pitcher built up to 400 throws a week is safer at 400 than an unconditioned one is at 250.

**Stated limitation:** the acute-to-chronic ratio has been criticised on methodological grounds and the specific cut points are conventions, not hard thresholds. Treat the ratio as a **flag that prompts a look**, not as a verdict. What it reliably catches is the case this system is most likely to produce — a movement-change block quietly doubling a guy's weekly throws because drill throws don't feel like work.

**Requirement:** the ratio needs 28 days of logged history before it means anything. Until then, hold weekly volume roughly flat and build the baseline.

**Roster-wide reference target:** alongside the ratio, a fixed weekly target of ~350 weighted throws is used as a simple secondary check — grounded in typical published in-season and velocity-block throwing volumes for this age group, not a hard ceiling. Treat it the same way as the ratio bands: a prompt to look, not an automatic cutoff.

- A distance reached once does not become the new floor.

### [throwing] 15.5a — Where high-intent long toss goes
It was missing from the prior draft. It is a **high-CNS I4 exposure** and it competes for the same weekly budget as a bullpen.

**Placement rules:**

1. **Off-season:** one high-intent long toss day per week, on a day that is already high-CNS. It pairs naturally with the weighted-implement session (§19.4) as the build-up into it — that is one exposure, not two.
2. **In-season, starter:** the day 3 or day 4 slot, furthest from the start in both directions. Never within 48 h of an outing.
3. **Never** on the same day as a bullpen, pulldowns, or a max-effort lift.
4. **Never** the day before a start.
5. It counts against the §12.2 weekly I4/I5 ceiling. For a starter in-season, the outing itself usually consumes that budget — meaning **high-intent long toss is mostly an off-season tool**, and in-season long toss should sit at I3.

**Why it earns a slot at all:** it is the highest-value velocity modality per unit of risk (§14.10). It builds arm speed through the whole chain, it is auto-regulated by the athlete's own movement quality rather than by a prescribed number, and unlike weighted implements it does not induce the ROM change that carries the injury signal.

### [throwing] 16.1 — Annual structure
Volume is expressed as a direction of travel rather than an absolute, per §15.2a — the controlling rule is the acute-to-chronic ratio, not a weekly ceiling.

| Period | Months | Status | Volume | Emphasis | Implements | Velocity work |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Second transition** | Late May–June | Restricted | **Zero.** Full shutdown, 2–4 weeks min. | None | None | None |
| **General preparatory** | June–July (or Sept without summer ball) | Building | Rebuild chronic load from zero. Ratio ≤1.3 every week. | Constrain, Time | 5 oz only | None |
| **Specific preparatory** | Aug–Oct | Developing | Steady build | Adapt, Move | 5 oz; cross-training implements in Phase 3 | Long toss building to I4 |
| **First transition** | Nov–Dec | Developing | Peak weekly volume | Bounce, Move, **Compete** | 4/5/6 oz per §6.6 | **Velocity block.** Pulldowns per §14.10. |
| **Competitive** | Feb–May | Competing | Held flat | Compete | 5 oz only | None |

**On the on-ramp out of shutdown:** coming off 2–4 weeks of zero, chronic load is zero and *any* throwing is an infinite ratio. Suspend the ratio rule for the first 3 weeks back and rebuild on a fixed schedule — roughly 25–30% of prior typical weekly volume in week one, adding about 25% per week — then let the ratio take over once 28 days of history exists.

**Compete moves into the first transition**, per §14.2. That is where a change gets tested at full intent while there is still time to respond to what you find.

### [throwing] 16.2 — The shutdown is not optional
Year-round throwing without an off period is one of the few injury risk multipliers entirely within your control. Two to four weeks of no throwing, minimum, once per year.

Athletes will resist this and will usually be the ones who feel best at the time. That is not evidence against it.

### [throwing] 17.2 — Red flags — stop throwing
Throwing stops immediately and does not resume until assessed:

- **Medial elbow pain.** Any. Treat as serious until proven otherwise.
- Pain that is sharp rather than sore
- Pain that persists more than 48 hours after an outing
- Pain accompanied by loss of velocity or loss of location
- Numbness or tingling into the hand
- A reported "pop," "give," or "slip"
- Significant loss of total shoulder rotational motion from baseline

A red flag sets throwing status to **Restricted** (§12.2), which zeroes the I4/I5 ceiling, and requires a coach reassessment before any throwing resumes.

### [throwing] 17.3 — The soreness distinction
Athletes will not volunteer this distinction, so it has to be asked for directly.

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| Front or back of the shoulder? | Routes to mechanics vs. imbalance |
| Inside or outside of the elbow? | **Inside is a red flag.** Outside is usually different. |
| Sore, or sharp? | Sore is normal adaptation. Sharp is not. |
| Did it show up during or after? | During is more concerning than after |
| Is it gone in 24 hours? 48? | Persistence past 48 h is a flag |

Build these five questions into the post-outing check-in rather than relying on "how's the arm?" — which reliably returns "fine."

### [throwing] 18.1 — The graded exposure model
**Step 1 — Graded Exposure.** Reintroduce load and range in small, controlled, monitored increments. The nervous system needs proof of safety before it will produce force.

**Step 2 — Active End-Range Control.** Regain *active*, not passive, control at the end ranges relevant to throwing. Passive range without active control at that range is not readiness.

**Step 3 — Sensorimotor Integration.** Reconnect proprioception and movement pattern under load, so the throwing action isn't relearned from scratch under pressure.

**Step 4 — Parasympathetic Pairing.** Pair exposure with recovery and down-regulation so the nervous system files new loads as safe rather than as threat. Methods in §18.4.

**Step 5 — Contextual Reintroduction.** Reintroduce the skill in the context it will be used in — mound, game speed, competition — **last**, and only after the first four hold.

### [throwing] 18.2 — The four phases
| Phase | Priority | Throwing | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Protective** | Tissue and joint protection | None | Pain-free range restored; general conditioning maintained |
| **Retraining** | Rebuild pattern and base strength without max intent | Graded exposure begins | Pattern reproducible at low intent; base strength markers returning |
| **Integration** | Reintroduce throwing-specific demand | Distance, intensity, complexity increase | Tolerates full distance and moderate intent with no next-day symptom |
| **Performance** | Full competitive intent | Full | Expressing trained qualities under game conditions, not merely tolerating load |

**Do not skip to Integration or Performance because an athlete feels good. Feeling good is a readiness signal, not a phase-completion signal.** This is the most common failure mode in return-to-throw and it is almost always athlete-driven.

### [throwing] 18.3 — Why effort percentages cannot control the dose
This is the section where §12.1a matters most.

Standard return-to-throw programs prescribe "50% effort" and "75% effort" as if those instructions reduced tissue load proportionally. They do not. At a stated 50% effort, elbow varus torque remains roughly 75–87% of maximum. A rehabbing athlete told to throw easy at 90 feet is loading his elbow at something close to three-quarters of his full-effort torque, on every throw.

**The controllable dose variables are:**

1. **Throw count** — the primary lever
2. **Distance** — shorter throws reduce load more reliably than softer throws do
3. **Rest interval** — between throws, between sessions
4. **Implement** — constrained-position plyo work loads differently than full-delivery throwing
5. **Position** — a constrained throwing position reduces *producible velocity*, which is not the same as reducing arm stress. Per §14.7, constrained positions do not proportionally unload the elbow. Use them for motor learning and count them at full weight; do not treat them as a dose reduction.

**Effort instruction is still given** — it buys mechanical control and keeps the athlete from testing the arm. It is just not the thing doing the dosing, and a program that treats it as such is not controlling what it thinks it's controlling.

### [throwing] 18.4 — Parasympathetic pairing — methods
Step 4 of the model had no methods attached to it in the prior document. These are the three:

**Diaphragmatic breathing.** Slow nasal breathing with a longer exhale than inhale, hand on belly to confirm the diaphragm rather than the chest is driving it. Increases vagal tone and shifts the athlete out of sympathetic dominance. Practically: 5 minutes immediately after each throwing exposure during Protective and Retraining phases.

**Progressive muscular relaxation.** Systematic tense-and-release through muscle groups, distal to proximal. Teaches the athlete to recognize and release residual tension — relevant because guarded, co-contracted throwing is both a re-injury pattern and a velocity problem (§1.2 on co-contraction).

**Systematic desensitization.** Graded exposure to the *anxiety* rather than the load. Build a hierarchy of throwing scenarios from least to most threatening — playing catch, flat ground, mound, mound with a hitter, game — and pair each one with the relaxation response before progressing. This is the direct counterpart to §18.1 Step 5 and it is the one most often skipped.

**Why this matters for a returning pitcher specifically:** the athlete's problem at the end of a rehab is frequently not tissue, it is that his nervous system has classified full-intent throwing as dangerous. Strength testing will not find that and neither will an MRI. It shows up as a guy who is cleared, healthy, and 4 mph down with no mechanical explanation.

### [throwing] 18.5 — Reintegration with the rest of the system
An athlete in Protective or Retraining is **Restricted** (§12.2): zero I4/I5, no high-intent throwing until cleared.

Lifting continues throughout, modified around the injured tissue. **Return-to-throw is not a reason to stop training the rest of the body**, and the athlete who detrains his lower half during a six-month elbow rehab returns with a smaller physical base than he left with.

Phase transitions require a real coach reassessment, not just symptom absence.
