## 1. Pitching biomechanics and movement analysis

### The Delivery, Phase by Phase: Research, Methods, and Fascia Training

### [strength] 1.1 — Movement analysis of pitching

**The trunk is the largest single contributor to ball velocity.** How the torso moves between foot strike and release is the most controllable variable available.

**Ground reaction force, measured:** lead leg peak vertical 1.3–2.0× bodyweight; drive leg 0.8–1.2× bodyweight.

### [strength] 1.2 — The two functional columns
 Every delivery splits the body into two columns doing opposite jobs. This distinction drives exercise selection and it drives how asymmetry findings are interpreted.

**ECCENTRIC COLUMN — absorbs, decelerates, resists.**

| Region | Structures |
 |---|---|
 | Lead leg | Quadriceps (braking at landing), hamstrings, glute max and medius, adductors, anterior tibialis |
 | Glove side | Glove-side scapular stabilizers, glove-side shoulder, glove-side obliques |
 | Trunk | Trunk decelerators, contralateral obliques, spinal erectors |
 | **Throwing arm — posterior** | **Posterior shoulder: infraspinatus, teres minor, posterior deltoid** |
 | **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side latissimus dorsi** |
 | **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side biceps brachii** (decelerates elbow extension) |
 | **Throwing arm** | **Throwing-side forearm musculature** (primary shock absorber at the elbow) |

This column is stressed by **braking**, not producing. It does its work after ball release, over roughly 50 milliseconds, and it is where most arm soreness and most deceleration-pattern injury originates.

**CONCENTRIC COLUMN — produces, transfers, accelerates.**

| Region | Structures |
 |---|---|
 | Trail leg | Glutes, hamstrings, quadriceps, **calves and plantar flexors** (drive and push-off) | | Hips and trunk | Hip rotators, ipsilateral obliques, spinal erectors into extension |
 | **Throwing shoulder** | Subscapularis, **pectoralis major and minor**, latissimus dorsi (as internal rotator), anterior deltoid |
 | **Throwing arm** | **Triceps brachii** (elbow extension), serratus anterior |
 | Forearm | Pronator teres, wrist flexors (terminal acceleration) |

### [biomechanics] 1.3 — Mobility and stability matrix by joint complex

#### Ankle complex

- **Closed-chain dorsiflexion mobility:** High positive carryover to velocity. Restricted dorsiflexion impairs lead-leg Ground Reaction Force (GRF) absorption at foot contact (Lin et al., 2024).
- **Ankle/calf base stability:** High impact on command. Prevents micro-slippage of the base of support, ensuring spatial release-point consistency.

#### Hip-pelvis complex

- **Lead hip internal rotation (IR):** Strong positive correlation with velocity (+10° lead hip IR correlates to ~1.3 mph / +0.6 m/s increase in fastball velocity; Solomito et al., 2024). Crucial for pelvic block efficiency.
- **Bilateral hip mobility and hinge stability:** Prevents early pelvic opening (arm-side/high misses) or late pelvic opening (glove-side/low misses).

#### Lumbo-pelvic and core complex

- **Isometric core stiffness (anti-rotation and anti-extension):** The core acts as an isometric torque transmitter rather than a dynamic generator. High Intra-Abdominal Pressure (IAP) and core stiffness prevent kinetic energy leakage between the pelvic block and upper trunk rotation.

#### Thoracic spine and scapulothoracic joint

- **Thoracic rotation and extension mobility:** High-velocity throwers exhibit ~55° ± 6° of trunk axial rotation at foot strike (Fleisig et al., 2023). Maximizes elastic loading via the diagonal fascial/oblique slings.
- **Scapular dynamic stability (upward rotation/protraction):** Critical for command. Scapular dyskinesis shifts the humerus's center of rotation, destroying release point repeatability and increasing elbow valgus torque.

#### Glenohumeral (GH) joint and rotator cuff

- **Passive external rotation (ER):** Moderate positive carryover via layback distance.
- **Rotator cuff eccentric strength:** Centers the humeral head during acceleration (~7,000–9,000°/sec). Muscular fatigue causes superior humeral head translation, leading to subacromial impingement and labral (SLAP) degradation.

### [biomechanics] 1.4 — Distal and peripheral musculature: health and command shields

- **Flexor-pronator mass (FPM):** Low velocity impact; **critical UCL shield and command engine**. FPM dynamic co-contraction counteracts peak elbow valgus torque (64–100+ Nm), which exceeds passive UCL tolerance (32–34 Nm). Controls seam orientation and spin axis at release.
- **Biceps brachii:** Low velocity impact; **critical deceleration engine**. Acts eccentrically post-release to prevent elbow hyperextension (>2,000°/sec). Prevents traction injuries to the superior labrum (SLAP tears).
- **Triceps brachii:** Moderate velocity impact via dynamic elbow extension. Governs terminal extension angle at release; regulates olecranon impaction to prevent posterior elbow impingement and Valgus Extension Overload (VEO).
- **Calf/ankle complex:** Provides closed-chain dorsiflexion braking at lead foot contact to stabilize the ground reaction base.

## 2. Physical qualities and energy-system demands

### [strength] 1.3 — Physiological analysis — ranked for pitching
 | Priority | Quality | Why |
 |---|---|---|
 | High | Rate of force development | The delivery is too short for peak force to be reached. How fast force rises is the constraint. |

| High | **Anaerobic power and capacity** | The primary energy system of the sport. See 1.4. A pitch is a maximal anaerobic effort, and an inning is a series of them. This is trained, not assumed. |
 | High | Eccentric / braking capacity | Front leg block and arm deceleration. Important for both upper and lower body. Before you can overcome force you must absorb it.

| High | Rotational power with rotational stability | Speed of rotation must be supported by the ability to stabilize it. Training raw rotational speed on an unstable base raises injury risk instead of transferring. |
 | Moderate | Maximal strength | A substrate for the above. Above a working threshold, returns diminish sharply. |

| Moderate | Aerobic capacity | Supports recovery between innings, between outings, and across a season. See 1.4 — this is a real quality, not a concession. |
 | Moderate | Hypertrophy | Matters for underweight and younger athletes and for connective tissue capacity. Not a standing goal for a filled-out college arm. |

| Low | Muscular endurance | Exception: forearm, cuff, and scapular tissue tolerance, programmed as tissue work rather than as a performance quality. |

### [strength] 1.4 — Energy system analysis
 **Anaerobic is the primary system and is trained as such.**

| System | Role in pitching | Training parameters |
 |---|---|---|
 | Phosphagen (ATP-PC) | One pitch. ~1.5 s of work with 15–25 s between efforts. | 90–100% intensity, 5–10 s efforts, 1:12 to 1:20 work:rest |
 | Fast glycolytic | An inning. Repeated maximal efforts with incomplete recovery. **This is the system that determines whether pitch 18 of an inning looks like pitch 3.** | 75–90% intensity, 15–30 s efforts, 1:3 to 1:5 work:rest |
 | Oxidative | Between innings, between outings, across a season. | 20–30% intensity, 3+ min efforts, 1:1 to 1:3 work:rest |

### [capacity] 2.1 — Exercise transfer and statistical correlations

Programming logic must prioritize Rate of Force Development (RFD) and rotational power over slow maximal strength.

| Exercise / performance test | Correlation to velocity (r) | Statistical significance | Programming and biomechanical classification |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Rotational Med Ball Throw (RMBTV)** | **r = 0.62** | p = 0.02 | **Tier 1 (Highest Transfer):** Explains ~38% of velocity variance. Direct SSC transfer of oblique slings. |
| **Loaded Jump Lunge (JLWL)** | **r = 0.369** | p < 0.05 | **Tier 1 (High Transfer):** 30-sec test using fixed 10kg load. Measures single-leg RFD and eccentric fatigue resistance. |
| **Short Acceleration (10yd / 10m)** | **r = -0.45 to -0.62** | p < 0.05 | **Tier 1 (High Transfer):** Matches ground contact time (40–60ms) and lead-leg braking impulse. |
| **Shoulder Internal Rotation (SIR)** | **r = -0.312** | p < 0.05 | Rotational mobility marker interacting with mechanical timing. |
| **1-RM Maximal Bench Press** | **r = 0.099** | p > 0.05 (N.S.) | **Low Direct Correlation:** Heavy 1-RM slow press does not predict speed alone. *Note: Explosive/ballistic pressing (40–60% load) increases velocity by +3.3 to +13.3 mph in intervention studies.* |

### [capacity] 2.2 — Energy systems and fatigue mechanics

- **ATP-PCr system:** Powers the single explosive throw (<2 seconds).
- **Aerobic capacity (VO2max):** Governs PCr resynthesis during the 15–30 sec recovery between pitches and 6–10 min between innings. Higher VO2max (48–55 mL/kg/min) strongly correlates with lower ERA/WHIP (r ≈ -0.68) and higher strike percentage (r = -0.864 with VO2 max fraction used).

#### Fatigue cascades

1. **Lower-leg fatigue** → Lead knee flexion post-contact increases by 4°–8° → Lead leg collapses → Velocity drops 1.5–3.0 mph → Release point shifts horizontally.
2. **Upper-body/scapular fatigue** → Loss of upward rotation → Arm lags behind trunk ("arm drag") → Peak elbow valgus torque spikes exponentially → Increased UCL tear risk.

### [strength] 4.5 — Exercise selection and order
 **Classes.** *Core* exercises are multi-joint and take priority. *Assistance* exercises are single-joint, generally corrective. *Structural* exercises load the spine directly or indirectly. *Power* exercises are structural movements performed explosively. *Recovery* exercises are low-stress and sit at the end of a session or in a session of their own.

**Order within a session:** power → core strength → assistance → recovery. Alternate upper/lower or push/pull to manage fatigue.

**Front-loading rule:** corrective, CNS, power, and agility work is performed **before** weight training and throwing, not after. Transfer is better when these are frontloaded.

**Muscle balance:** the agonist should not significantly outpace the antagonist. For pitchers this is specifically the accelerator/decelerator relationship at the shoulder, which is why posterior shoulder and scapular work is standing content rather than a phase.

**Cross-pattern selection:** force transfers contralaterally — for a right-handed pitcher, right pec and shoulder girdle work with left oblique, hip flexor, and quad, while left lat and right glute work together. A useful lens when selecting anti-rotation and rotational accessory work.

### [strength] 4.6 — Frequency — the CNS budget
 Frequency is not set by lifting alone. **The governing number is total high-CNS exposures per week, and throwing counts.**

**High-CNS:** max-effort lifting (RPE 9+), sprinting, extensive plyometrics, depth jumps and KEAT, ballistic lower-body work, any high-intent throwing day, game appearances.

**Low-CNS:** submaximal lifting (RPE ≤7), crawling and positional work, moderate horizontal jumps, mobility, low-intensity isometrics, zone 2 aerobic work, recovery sessions.

**Standing rules:**

1. Never stack two high-CNS days back to back.
 2. Throwing days are high-CNS days when planning lifting.
 3. High-CNS lifting does not precede a high-intent throwing day.
 4. **An athlete's team lift heavy day is a high-CNS exposure we do not control.** Ask once, store it on the athlete record, treat it as fixed for the season, and program around it.
 5. Weekly high-CNS ceiling: 3 off-season, 2 in-season (which for most athletes is consumed entirely by throwing and team lifting).

### [strength] 4.7 — One primary quality per phase
 A phase develops one primary quality. Everything else is maintained, not developed. Two primary qualities produces two half-adaptations and a fatigued athlete.

The primary quality is chosen from the 1.3 ranking and the force-velocity profile from 2.3, and does not change until the mesocycle boundary (2–6 weeks, standard 4) — testing and quality changes happen at block boundaries only, never mid-block.

### [strength] 4.8 — Progression, deload, and regression
 **The RPE 2-for-2 rule.** If a prescribed RPE 8 set comes back at RPE 6 or lower for two consecutive sessions, increase load: 2–5% upper body, 5–10% lower body. The reverse also holds — RPE 10 on a prescribed 8 for two consecutive sessions drops the load and triggers a deload check.

**Deload triggers.** Any two in the same week:

- CMJ height down ≥5% from rolling baseline
 - **RSImod down ≥10% from rolling baseline** (more sensitive than height; often fires first) - Prescribed loads coming in 2+ RPE points higher than programmed
 - Resting heart rate elevated ≥5 bpm over baseline for 3+ days
 - Reported sleep disruption across multiple nights
 - Reported loss of appetite or persistent mood disturbance
 - Arm feeling heavy or slow during warm-up on consecutive throwing days
 - Velocity down with no mechanical change identified

## 4. Annual plan, season phases, and adaptation

### [strength] 5.2 — The governing theory
 **Stimulus–fatigue–recovery–adaptation.** The greater the workload, the greater the fatigue and the longer the delay before adaptation appears. As fatigue dissipates, performance rises above baseline. If no new stimulus arrives, detraining follows.

**Fitness–fatigue.** Every session produces both fitness and fatigue. Preparedness is the difference between them. High loads raise fitness *and* fatigue simultaneously, so preparedness drops before it rises. This is why a peak cannot be held: peak performance is sustainable for 1–2 weeks, and attempting to hold it longer produces either fitness loss or overtraining.

**Overtraining continuum:**

| State | Duration | Signs | Response | |---|---|---|---|

| Acute fatigue | 1–2 days | None, or improved performance | Normal. Continue. |
 | Functional overreaching | Days to 1 week | Temporary performance dip | Deload week resolves it. |
 | Non-functional overreaching | Weeks to months | Stagnant or declining performance, mood disturbance, degraded coordination | Extended unload. Reassess the whole block. |
 | Overtraining syndrome | Months to years | Dramatic performance loss, frequent illness | Refer out. Not a programming problem at this point. |

Causes: large jumps in volume or intensity, and **training monotony** — the same stimulus repeated without variation, which is the failure mode most common in remote programming.

### [strength] 5.5 — Non-linear structure for advanced athletes
 This roster is advanced, so linear progression within a block is exhausted. Within a microcycle, vary intensity: one heavy day and one speed day in the same week rather than progressing both together. Light days carry roughly 80% of the heavy day's load.

**Block structure ⚙:** standard mesocycle is 4 weeks — three loading weeks with an ascending profile, then a deload. Deload week drops volume to roughly 50% and holds intensity, which sheds fatigue without shedding fitness.

## 5. Training methods and modalities

### [strength] 6.1 — Eccentric training **Classifications:**

| Class | Load | Method | Available remotely |
 |---|---|---|---|
 | Submaximal | 60–90% of concentric max | Slow eccentrics, tempo work, isometric/eccentric contrast | Yes |
Percentages are of the athlete's **concentric** 1RM for that lift.

**Remote default:** tempo eccentrics at submaximal load. A 4-second lowering phase at 70% produces real eccentric stimulus with no partner requirement and no failure exposure. This is the workhorse and it should be the default prescription.

**Programming:** 1–2 eccentric-emphasis exercises per session, in the strength phases. Eccentric work carries a long recovery cost — 48–72 hours minimum before the same pattern is loaded again, and it does not belong in the week before a start.

### [strength] 6.2 — Isometric training
 | Type | Application |
 |---|---|
 | Overcoming | Push against an immovable object. Maximal neural drive. |
 | Yielding | Hold against gravity. Strength-endurance and position tolerance. |

| Classification | Prescription | Purpose | |---|---|---|

| Max duration | 20–60 s at 50–80% | Hypertrophy, tissue tolerance | | Max intensity | 6–10 s at maximal intent | Neural drive |
 | Ballistic | 1–2 s explosive effort | RFD |

**Ramp-up isometric protocol, applied to throwing positions:**

| Tier | Effort | Hold | Reps | Purpose | |---|---|---|---|---|
 | 1 | 50% | 15 s | 2 | Prime and perfuse | | 2 | 75% | 7 s | 2 | Cross the threshold | | 3 | 90% | 4 s | 2 | Near-max intent |

15–30 seconds rest between reps. Applied to each throwing position: layback (ER), release point (IR), elbow flexion, elbow extension — all at 90 degrees of abduction, with the release-point position done for IR only.

Isometrics are low-CNS and can be programmed on days that would otherwise be off. They are the most useful modality for the E3 equipment tier.

### [strength] 6.3 — Ballistic training
 **Load:** 10–25% of max for projected implements. The load is projected rather than decelerated, which is the defining feature.

**Methods:** med ball throws, jump squats, weighted jumps, rotational throws.

**Governing rule:** implement speed is the prescription. The set ends when speed drops, not when the rep count is met.

**Volume:** 3–6 sets of 3–5 reps per exercise. Full recovery between sets. Ballistic work done under fatigue trains the wrong thing.

### [strength] 6.4 — Plyometric training
 **Definition:** maximum force in the shortest possible time. Trains the stretch-shortening cycle, the series elastic components, the tendons, and the stretch reflex.

**The stretch-shortening cycle:**

1. **Eccentric phase** — the agonist is stretched, elastic energy is stored in the series elastic components
 2. **Amortization phase** — the transition. Signals transmit to the agonist. **The longer this phase, the more stored energy dissipates as heat.** Amortization time is the whole game.

3. **Concentric phase** — muscle shortens, elastic energy is released

**Throwing is itself a plyometric action.** Plyometric throwing volume counts against plyometric volume caps below.

**Volume caps — foot contacts or throws per session:**

| Training status | Contacts/session | |---|---|
 | Beginner | 80–100 |
 | Intermediate | 100–120 |

| Advanced | 120–140 |

**Block length:** 6–10 weeks. Progressively increase frequency, volume, intensity, and exercise type across the block. Plyometrics are a block modality, not standing content.

**Pairing rule:** combine lower-body lifting with upper-body plyometrics, and upper-body lifting with lower-body plyometrics. Heavy lifting and plyometrics for the same region on the same day is complex training — it works, but it demands recovery this roster's throwing volume does not leave room for. **Not prescribed in-season.**

### [strength] 6.5 — KEAT and shock training
 **Kinetic Energy Accumulation Training** uses falling or absorbed forces to develop supramaximal eccentric force. Methods: depth jumps, altitude landings, overspeed eccentrics with bands or releases.

**Why it stays in the system.** KEAT trains force absorption more directly and at higher magnitude than a clean catch does, and it is the primary trainer of braking rate of force development — the eccentric column's defining quality and one of the better predictors of pitching velocity in the jump literature.

> **"Before you can overcome force, you must absorb it."**

**Progression ⚙:**

| Step | Drop height | Advancement criterion |
 |---|---|---|
 | 1 | Altitude landing only (no rebound), 12" | 3 sessions, quiet landing on video, no valgus |
 | 2 | Depth jump, 12" | RSI stable or improving across 2 sessions |
 | 3 | Depth jump, 18" | RSI stable or improving across 2 sessions; G4 for anything above |
 | 4 | Depth jump, 24" | Bodyweight under 220 lb; RSI must **improve** over the 18" value or the athlete stays at 18" |

**Hard limits:**

- Bodyweight over 220 lb: do not exceed 18 inches.
 - Ground contact time above 250 ms means the athlete is absorbing rather than rebounding — drop the height.
 - RSI that falls when height rises means the height is too high. **The optimal drop height is the tallest box at which RSI is still improving, and it is individual.** This is why RSI is worth measuring.
 - Maximum 40 contacts per session for depth jumps specifically, counted inside the 6.4 volume cap.

**Placement:** first transition phase (November–December) and, if the calendar allows, a shorter block in the general preparatory period. **Not in-season.**

### [strength] 6.6 — Weighted implement throwing
 **Load range: ±20% of a regulation 5 oz baseball. That means 4 oz and 6 oz. Nothing outside that window.**

**Testing protocol.** Establish min, max, and average velocity for each implement:

| Implement | Throws |
 |---|---|
 | 5 oz baseball | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE | | 6 oz | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE |
 | 5 oz baseball | 2 @ 100% RPE |
 | 4 oz | 1 @ 80% RPE, 2 @ 100% RPE |

**Training protocol.** Same sequence, 3–4 throws at 100% RPE per implement. **Terminate the session early when velocity drops 4% below the tested average.** This is the auto-regulation rule and it is not optional — it is what keeps a velocity session from becoming a fatigue session.

**Frequency:** 2–3× per 14 days in a velocity block. Never in-season for a starter without removing an equivalent high-intent exposure elsewhere.

### [strength] 6.7 — Rotational work
 **Stability precedes speed.** Speed of rotation must be supported by the ability to stabilize rotation first. Training raw rotational speed without adequate core and base stability increases injury risk rather than transferring to velocity. Anti-rotation work is the entry requirement for rotational power work, not an accessory to it.

**Progression:** anti-rotation (pallof press, bird dog) → controlled rotation under load (chops, lifts) → rotational power (med ball throws) → rotational power from throwing-specific positions.

**Diagonal chop:** performed across a progression of increasingly demanding stance positions that mimic release stance. Trains the body to stabilize the speeds and forces produced in the arms during rotation. Cable at E1/E2 where available; band substitution at E2/E3 is mandatory rather than optional.

### 5.1 Long-duration isometrics

6.8 Long-duration isometrics

Yielding holds at long muscle length: split squat at 90/90, deep push-up, lengthened hamstring hold, wall sit, dead hang.

Accumulate 60 s to 5 min per position, 1–3 positions per session. Fail downward into the lengthened position, never up and out.

Nose breathing, about 4 s in and 8 s out. After a break, 1–2 nose breaths, then back in.

Builds position tolerance and muscle. Not tendon or velocity work; pair with §6.2 max overcoming isometrics for tendon. Low neural cost, high local fatigue. None within 24 h before a bullpen for the region trained. 2–3 times a week in general prep; once a week, 90 s or less, in-season.

### 5.2 Light-load rapid reversals

6.9 Light-load rapid reversals

Load of 10% or less, or a tennis ball, dowel, empty bar, or light band.

Reverse at end range as fast as possible, driving the return with the antagonist. 10–25 reps or 10–30 s per set, 2–4 sets.

End the set when the movement visibly slows.

Lower body and trunk: open at gate G1 (cleared for loaded jumps).

Throwing shoulder ER/IR at 90/90: tennis-ball load only, gate T1 (cleared for high-intent throwing), none within 48 h of a start.

Counts as arm-care volume, not throws.

May follow a 5–7 s overcoming isometric in the same position.

### 5.3 Ballistic-intent isometrics

Ballistic-intent isometrics as arm-free speed work Mechanism 1. Behm and Sale's dose was 5 sets of 10 explosive attempts, 3 days a week. Use 1–2 second all-out attempts in throwing positions against a strap or rack: IR at 90/90, elbow extension, lead-leg block. Good for in-season weeks with no room for I4 throwing (high intent), and for the Retraining phase of return to throw. They still load the cuff, so keep volume low and track them as arm-care volume.

### 5.4 EVO methods and related research

EVO Core methods:

Extreme isometrics Long holds, most famously a 5-minute lunge at 90/90. As fatigue sets in, sink deeper into the lengthened position rather than rising out of it.

Overcoming isometrics

Max-intent push or pull against something that can't move

Extreme slows Very slow eccentrics, e.g. a 30-second squat descent driven by the hamstrings

Drops, catches, rebounds Drop from height or release a load, catch it, reverse immediately

Rapid response, RDC, impulses Very light loads moved with max-speed reversals; rope impulses

Daily structure: extreme isos first, then drops and rebounds

A muscle can contract only as fast as its antagonist relaxes. Holding a position cycles agonist and antagonist rapidly, so isometrics are "velocity training." Failing downward keeps the muscle lengthening and trains every joint angle along the way. The 5-minute mark is rhetorical. Fatiguing several times inside a hold matters more than the clock. Stop a set once speed falls about 3–4% from max; slower reps rehearse a slower pattern.

EVO:

Once the holds are possible, add load and work back to 5 minutes, up to 33–40% of bodyweight…slow lowers every day, plus a heavy rebound bench and rebound squat every day…a potent stimulus done daily, rather than the usual two or three times a week…Rapid reversals: motor-unit doublets Twelve weeks of fast contractions at 30–40% of max raised the share of motor units firing "doublets" (two impulses 2–5 ms apart) from 5.2% to 32.7%. Units also switched on earlier and fired faster…

Pull into position": turning a hold into a push Isometrics come in two kinds. Holding a load in place (yielding) failed about twice as fast as pushing against something fixed: 19 s versus 41 s at the same effort. (Schaefer & Bittmann 2017) Holding is a position-control task; pushing is a force-control task. Brief voluntary pulses during a hold shift it toward pushing. (Schaefer 2021) A 2024 review found holding resembles the control used to lengthen a muscle, and pushing resembles shortening

Extreme isos to failure: size, not strength Light loads taken to failure built about the same muscle as heavy loads, but heavy loads raised 1RM (one-rep max) more. (Schoenfeld 2017) With light loads, reaching failure matters; with heavy loads it matters less. (summary) That explains Schroeder's line that athletes "become excessively stronger and bigger" from long holds. The bigger part is well supported; the stronger part depends on how strength is measured.

### 5.5 Marinovich: light-load speed, coordination, and braking

MARINOVICH:

Light, fast loads transfer widely In an elbow-flexor study, training at 35% of 1RM raised power at every load tested from 15% to 90%, and still raised 1RM. Training at 90% helped mostly near that load. Loads near peak-power output gave the broadest transfer

But not to failure A Japanese study of judo athletes compared light ballistic training to failure, light ballistic training stopped short, and heavy training to failure. Training to failure built strength and muscle but not power.

Speed and the brake are planned together A fast single-joint movement runs on three bursts: the agonist fires to accelerate, the antagonist fires to brake, and the agonist fires again to steady the end. (J Physiol) The antagonist activity needed to stop a movement rises in a straight line with peak speed. Fast, short movements need the brake earlier. (Marsden, Obeso, Rothwell 1983) The brake is prepared and triggered as its own step, close to when it is needed, not fired automatically by the first burst. (UBC thesis) Co-contraction rises with training and with high-speed movement, probably to protect joints rather than to add speed. (Behm & Sale review) So "relaxation" isn't the absence of the antagonist. It is the antagonist arriving at the right moment and staying quiet until then.

### 5.6 Plyometric and shock-method research

The original shock-method dosing Depth jumps from 0.75 m, or up to 1.10 m when the goal is max strength. (N. Verkhoshansky) 4 sets of 10, about 40 takeoffs per session. Western coaches of the era thought it was too light. (Bret Contreras interview) Blocks of 3–4 weeks, with at least 4 weeks between blocks. (EliteFTS, citing Roman 1986) If the heels touch down on landing, the drop is too high. (same source) Verkhoshansky's depth jump is not Bosco's drop jump. The drop jump runs 20–60 cm and controls contact time. The depth jump aims for max rebound height toward an overhead target. Verkhoshansky reported trained volleyball players gaining 14% in max strength from a depth-jump program. (Breaking Muscle)

**Phosphagen work is the priority and sprinting is the best tool for it.** It is the system the sport actually runs on, and it is trained with maximal intent and full recovery. Cutting the rest interval converts it into glycolytic work and defeats the purpose.

**Anaerobic power and capacity** is the quality that determines whether the eighteenth pitch of an inning looks like the third. Acceptable modes: sled pushes, KB swings, rower, submaximal squat or deadlift circuits, thrusters, bike sprints. Rotate modes to avoid monotony. A representative session: 30 s on / 30 s off for 8 minutes.

**Zone 2** is programmed at minimum effective dose, not excluded. 20–45 minutes at conversational pace, 2–3× per week, on low-CNS days. Modes: bike, row, incline walk, easy jog, **poles.** There is nothing wrong with poles as a mode. The historical error was volume and placement, not the activity.

### 9.1 Weighted-ball research and throwing periodization

Building the Throwing Program: Soviet Methods, Weighted Balls, and Periodization

Executive summary The Soviet throws coaches worked out most of the rules decades ago: vary implement weight by 5–20%, throw two modified implements for every standard one, and change the exercise once the athlete reaches form. Baseball research mostly confirms them, with one big addition: the main way weighted balls add velocity is also the main way they add risk. On your 4–7 oz question: pitched from a mound, 4, 5, 6, and 7 oz balls did not raise arm torque per throw; torque actually fell as weight rose. But heavier balls immediately increase shoulder external rotation (layback), and that gain is both the likeliest source of the velocity and the likeliest source of injury. The velocity training studies that worked used 4–6 oz, which is the Soviet ±20% window. My recommendation: keep 4–6 oz as the default, treat 7 oz as optional and mound-only, and never use weighted balls in flat-ground run-and-gun throws, which raise elbow torque. On whether frequent high-intensity throwing is necessary: some is, because speed adapts to speed. But the protocols that worked used about three sessions a week for 6–10 weeks, not daily max effort, and much of the gain in several studies came simply from throwing real balls in volume. No study shows towel or dry throwing alone raises velocity; the towel shifts the release point and unloads the arm, so it's a pattern and recovery tool, not a velocity stimulus. On stagnation: Soviet theory names two forces in tension. Accommodation says a constant stimulus loses its effect; directed adaptation says a stimulus must be kept long enough to work. Bondarchuk resolved it with 4–8 week exercise blocks, changed at the peak rather than after a stall. Foster's monotony index gives you a number to watch week to week.

## 10. Scientific program-design research

Scientific Program Design and Long-Term Adaptation

What the evidence supports well: Load determines the adaptation. Across 295 studies, maximal strength rose most with the heaviest loads, jump height with about 30% of one-rep max (1RM), and power with 40–70%. (Swinton 2024) Resistance training raises throwing velocity. A meta-analysis of 16 controlled trials found a large effect, and barbell compound lifts have the strongest evidence. (Martínez-García 2021, Hadjisavvas 2024) One session a week maintains strength in season, if the load stays heavy. (Rønnestad 2011, Spiering 2021) Stopping sets well short of failure keeps strength and protects speed. Velocity loss doesn't change strength gains, but higher loss hurts jumping and sprinting. (Jukic 2022) Perceived throwing effort overstates the reduction in arm load. A "50% effort" throw still produced 75–87% of max elbow torque. (Melugin 2019, Fiegen 2023)

How often to lift Development. In elite rugby and football players, two to four sessions per muscle group per week built strength; weekly strength gains were about 0.9% at two sessions, 1.8% at three, and 1.3% at four. (McMaster 2013) Frequency matters less when volume is equal. In well-trained people over 6–12 weeks, splitting the same volume across more or fewer sessions made no clear difference to strength. (Cuthbert 2021) A larger meta-regression found strength rose slightly with frequency, with diminishing returns, and hypertrophy didn't. (Pelland 2025) Maintenance. One session a week preserved strength and sprint speed for 12 weeks in pro soccer players; one every other week didn't. (Rønnestad 2011) In general populations, one session a week with one set per exercise held strength for up to 32 weeks if load stayed heavy. (Spiering 2021) What "high demand" really means In trained men, ten sets of heavy squats, ten sets of jump squats, and fifteen 30 m sprints each caused fatigue that cleared in 48 hours (jumps) or 72 hours (squats and sprints). Most of it was in the muscle; nervous-system drive dipped for only 24–48 hours. (Thomas 2018) Training to failure slows recovery by roughly 24–48 hours compared with stopping short at the same volume. (Morán-Navarro 2017) So your "CNS day" rule is right about the spacing and wrong about the reason. A more accurate label is a high-demand day: high mechanical load, high tissue load, and maximal intent. Conditioning without losing power Distance running costs power. In Wilson's meta-analysis, power gains were roughly halved by concurrent endurance work, more with running than cycling and more with longer, more frequent sessions. (Wilson 2012) Page 7 of 23 Salzman Baseball: Scientific Program Design and Long-Term Adaptation Short sprints don't. Sprint-interval training added aerobic fitness with no loss of strength, jump, or sprint gains, and sprints of 10 seconds or less may help jumping. (Guimarães 2025) Longer high-intensity intervals did reduce jump gains. (Kikuchi 2026) Order. Lifting before endurance work favored lower-body strength. (Murlasits 2018) Is high/low organization supported? Partly. The recovery data support separating hard days by about 48 hours, and grouping sprinting, jumping, heavy lifting, and high-intent throwing onto the same day protects the days between. But no trial has compared grouping against spreading these out, and no study tests concurrent throwing and lifting in pitchers. The structure is defensible; it is a coaching framework, not an established optimum.

Exercise selection and session structure No study has tested an ideal number of exercises per session. Research measures sets, load, and effort, so exercise count is best treated as an output of those decisions rather than a target. What the volume research shows Strength plateaus early; size keeps rising slowly. Across 67 studies, more weekly sets raised both strength and hypertrophy, but strength gains flattened much sooner. Counting indirect sets as half a set predicted results best. (Pelland 2025) Trained men got similar strength from 1, 3, or 5 sets per exercise, three times a week for 8 weeks, in sessions as short as 13 minutes. Muscle size favored more sets. (Schoenfeld 2018) Proximity to failure matters for size, not strength. Strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve; hypertrophy improved closer to failure. (Robinson 2024) Page 9 of 23 Salzman Baseball: Scientific Program Design and Long-Term Adaptation Minimal effective dose for strength in trained lifters: about 3–6 hard sets of 1–5 reps per lift per week above 80% 1RM, plus 1–3 accessories per main lift. (Androulakis- Korakakis 2021) This comes from powerlifters, so it's an extrapolation for pitchers. What the specificity research shows Barbell compound lifts had the strongest evidence for raising throwing velocity among handball players; band, medicine-ball, and core work had too few studies to judge. (Hadjisavvas 2024) Hip strength is a well-established predictor of pitch velocity in adult pitchers. (Manzi 2023) Shoulder internal-rotation strength and shoulder flexion range went with lower elbow torque at the same velocity in Division I pitchers. (Barrack 2024) Arm care. External-rotation strengthening cut injuries to 9.8% versus 22.6% with sleeper stretching in high school pitchers. (Shitara 2022) A multi-part warm-up program nearly halved shoulder and elbow injuries in youth players. (Sakata 2019) A band-and-stretch program didn't reduce shoulder injuries in 579 handball players. (Achenbach 2022)
